## Research: fact-checking evaluations may mix in future evidence unknowable at the time of the claim

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04888) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/of8dpno9hcfswb26ubo3vxvru)

Researchers reconstructed the evidence conditions at the time of claim creation on AVeriTeC and QuanTemp++, and probed model representations for encoded outcome information, finding clear parametric leakage that is masked by overall accuracy. The authors state that a simple representation bottleneck reduces such future information leakage more effectively than mutual information-based training penalties. The study also found that allowing evidence published after the claim inflates AVeriTeC zero-shot accuracy by 6.3 percentage points, while the effect is negligible on QuanTemp++, where retrieval provides almost no post-hoc evidence. The authors conclude that misinformation benchmarks overestimate fact-checking performance if they do not account for information actually available at the time of the claim.

### Questions

Fact-checking benchmarks often count information unknowable at the time of the claim as success. The parameter channel is the outcome memorized during pretraining; the temporal channel is retrieved evidence published after the claim. The authors argue that a single accuracy score cannot distinguish these two paths.

### Methods

Recover the publication date on AVeriTeC and QuanTemp++, split into point-in-time and leaked views. Entity obfuscation tests the parameter channel, with the control being GPT-2-XL truncated at December 2017. On frozen Qwen3-14B, compare width bottleneck against CLUB penalty.

### Experiments and results

Qwen3-14B after occlusion: Supported recall 0.421→0.131, accuracy drops 0.032; control reverses. AVeriTeC post-hoc evidence raises zero-shot by 6.3 points; with prior evidence +0.031. QuanTemp++ difference 0.05% (p=0.87).

### Limitations and future work

The authors state that outcome information cannot be said to be completely removed: date coverage is incomplete, no manual spot checks were done, and the two date tools differ. The training detector uses only Qwen3-14B, with features processed via mean-pooling and PCA. Next, they will test claims after the training cutoff and check whether high-quality evidence exists before the claim.

## RSI-Anything: Unlocking Recursive Self-Improvement with World Models

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13912) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/hs7x9nwt4upy3dgns638977ox)

The paper proposes RSI-Anything, a domain-general framework built on JEPA-Anything, which uses predictive world models to guide action and training data selection for recursive self-improvement; observed feedback drives policy or workflow improvements and conditional world model adaptation, while component and compatibility checks determine whether to retain the updated policy-model combination. In code repair tasks, with a fixed coder, the success rate improves from 26.3% with a static verifier to 29.2% with predictive selection, reaching 31.2% after world model adaptation, with comparable test usage. In GUI evaluation, task completion rates are higher, with average action count reduced by 39.4%; laboratory planning scores more than double from 21.80% to 46.01% across five consecutive policy versions. In reaction optimization, under the same budget of 40 experiments, RSI-Anything achieves an average optimal yield of 96.15%, compared to 82.16% for Bayesian optimization. The paper also presents an end-to-end immunology application, connecting workflow evolution and predictive modeling to therapeutic hypothesis generation.

### Questions

Recursive self-improvement requires one round of interaction experience to improve the next round's actions and learning, and the updates must be retained. External feedback requires executing the candidate before the consequences are known, and the budget is limited; policy updates also change the candidate distribution the world model faces.

### Methods

RSI-Anything uses the world model from JEPA-Anything to score candidates and decide who gets external evaluation. Feedback drives policy or workflow updates, and adapts the world model when reliability, data, and budget allow; component checks and compatibility checks decide which pair is kept.

### Experiments and results

With the coder fixed, CodeContests hidden-test all-pass rates go from 26.3% to 31.2% (static verifier to world-model adaptation). Five versions of 4B F200 Level-2 go from 21.80% to 46.01%. Across 40 streaming experiments, the average best yield is 96.15%, versus 82.16% for the control.

### Limitations and future work

The authors say that joint multi-round evaluation of the policy and world model within the same task is still needed; the lab results evaluate protocol-aligned planning, and the treatment hypothesis awaits experimental testing. The code fix keeps the coder fixed, and OSWorld does not report absolute success rates.

## Paper uses generative agents to model how private test sets affect the gap between scores and user experience

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09296) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/fw1b4bwqs4596pq2vfdhzvbro)

An arXiv paper proposes a simulation architecture combining rule-based market dynamics with LLM-driven policy agents to study how benchmark design affects decisions by model providers, users, funders, and regulators. The authors model benchmarks, consumer demand, and provider capabilities as vectors over six capability dimensions: reasoning, programming, knowledge, safety, communication, and agents, and use benchmark held-out set design as a case study. The simulation finds that shifting from public benchmarks to private held-out benchmarks narrows the gap between scores and user satisfaction on most benchmarks, but widens it on a few, depending on which capabilities the held-out weights attribute scores to. The authors say the simulation can serve as a hypothesis-generation sandbox for studying how evaluation and policy choices reshape the ecosystem in turn.

### Questions

Benchmark scores guide R&D, capital, and regulation, but design methods often fix the rest of the ecosystem. The authors argue that evaluation should be treated as a closed loop, asking what the ecosystem does after a benchmark exists, using whether the holdout set is public as a case study.

### Methods

The authors build a hybrid generative multi-agent model. A six-dimensional vector represents capabilities, benchmark loadings, and consumer demand; LLMs only make monthly institutional allocations, while scoring and market use rules, and information is forced into three parts: public, private, and truth. The case compares five holdout settings.

### Experiments and results

The authors report that from fully public to fully private, the absolute gap in LLM runs narrowed in 10 of 13 benchmarks and widened in 3, and this held for 10/10 seeds; for the heuristic it narrowed in 9/13. The authors state that widening occurred when the held-out weights shifted scoring credit toward dimensions where R&D was already concentrated.

### Limitations and future work

The authors state that the mechanism depends on weight perturbation, a six-dimensional ontology, leaderboard competition, and model-defined satisfaction; dimension loadings are settings. The simulation does not predict real markets and needs to be tested on real leaderboards. The heuristic uses 50 seeds per condition, Sonnet 10, and Opus and GPT-5.5 each 3.

## RATED: Reliability-Aware Tool Retrieval for AI Agents via Execution Dynamics

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13889) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/j60gmjz75xb72a7mwwidy8yh0)

RATED is a tool retrieval framework for AI agents that combines semantic matching with dynamic reliability adaptation, using a lightweight contextual bandit module to rerank candidates based on real-time execution feedback, adapting to runtime tool failures without retraining. On the EcoAgent benchmark with 5,172 real tools, its MRR improves by 52.2% relative to the ToolLLM API retriever, and under severe API failures with a 30% failure rate, the failure selection rate drops from 86.2% to 2.9%, with additional latency under 1 ms.

### Questions

The agent must select tools from a growing tool library that remain usable at execution time. Existing retrieval mainly optimizes semantics; the authors claim that methods updating the planner with batch trajectories cannot change ranking between adjacent requests. The authors claim that over 50% of tools in four platform catalogs have broken links or lack authentication.

### Methods

RATED uses a contrastively fine-tuned and frozen DRAGON dual encoder to take the top-10 by semantic score, then reranks with a per-tool discounted LinUCB based on binary feedback from single executions and a metadata prior. EcoAgent contains 5,172 tools, and synthetic queries are generated by Gemma-3-1b.

### Experiments and results

Under 30% failure injection, RATED's FSR is 2.9%, and ToolLLM's is 86.2%. On EcoAgent-Real, the authors report a 52.2% MRR improvement relative to ToolLLM, but 0.618 is lower than Ada-002's 0.670. The extra latency is 0.172 ms.

### Limitations and future work

Conclusion leaves cold-start evaluation of newly added tools and live agent runtime validation as future work. Experiments include EcoAgent offline retrieval, and dynamic selection under simulated persistent failures at 20% and 30%. The paper does not report the number of repetitions for the dynamic experiments.

## FAITH: A feasibility-aware safety filtering reinforcement learning framework for high-dimensional systems

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12432) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/d4pxfhyhnqsfgy3qgltwlqu7h)

FAITH is a model-free, feasibility-aware safe filtering reinforcement learning framework that uses a feedforward network to approximate the optimal state-action safety value and amortizes minimal intervention filtering, allowing the task policy to optimize reward through the filtered dynamics without adding competing safety terms in policy updates. On a double integrator, Safety Gym, and a 29-DoF humanoid robot, FAITH achieves no violations and the highest reward from feasible starting points, reaching a 99.95% safety rate in Walking-Avoid while retaining 97% of the unfiltered reward, and achieving the highest safety rate in Push-Avoid by sacrificing balance and falling away from the protected zone; the same policy is also demonstrated on a real Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

### Questions

Safe reinforcement learning often places safety and task return in the same policy objective, and updates may cancel each other out. Classical filters require analytic safety functions and dynamics and only minimize instantaneous action deviation; projection is undefined when no safe action exists. High-dimensional contact systems also need to distinguish whether violations can ultimately be avoided.

### Methods

FAITH uses a backup actor and safety critic to approximate the optimal state-action peak harm, then amortizes minimal intervention with a feedforward residual network. The task policy uses PPO to maximize task reward only on filtered dynamics. When the acceptance set is empty, the same loss prioritizes lower predicted peak harm, without switching to a recovery controller.

### Experiments and results

On feasible starting points in the double integrator and Safety Gym, FAITH achieves 100% safety rate, and the authors claim the highest reward among violation-free methods. Walking-Avoid safety rate 99.95%, the authors claim retaining 97% of unfiltered reward; Push-Avoid safety rate 85.45%, the highest measured.

### Limitations and future work

The authors note that with discounting, finite λ, and learning errors, there are no formal safety or optimality guarantees; evaluation is limited to planar protected zones, excluding impact severity and human injury. Hardware demonstrations are qualitative, with no safety or harm statistics. Simulation covers the double integrator, SafetyCarCircle1-v0, and two 29-DoF tasks.

## Using subsequence association filtering and breadth-aware selection to suppress hallucinated words in LLM query expansion

[Original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13810) · [Read on the website](https://aisafetyhot.com/en/items/d9mkvhcgpxmelgwjo2u0g2wc0)

SAFE-QE suppresses hallucinated words in LLM query expansion through subsequence association filtering and breadth-aware selection. On TREC and BEIR, its nDCG@10 is not statistically different from generative and corpus-guided expansion, but it injects 4.3 unsupported words per query, while Query2doc and HyDE inject 27.8 and 57.5. The method is training-free and retriever-agnostic, scores candidate words by cross-context reproducibility conditioned on the corpus, down-weights words that recur under random contexts but lack support from pseudo-relevance sets, and proposes the Hallucinated Expansion Term Rate to measure query-side faithfulness.

### Questions

LLM query expansion can inject entities unsupported by the corpus and off-topic words, increasing ranking noise and potentially harming strong retrievers. Existing filtering typically keeps or discards words based on co-occurrence with retrieval evidence, and how many words to keep is left to fixed top-k or tuned weights.

### Methods

SAFE-QE does not train. After sampling candidate terms with an open instruction-tuned LLM, it scores them using reproducibility across random contexts and support from pseudo-relevant documents, penalizing terms that are highly reproducible and unsupported; then it greedily maximizes coverage under grounding constraints and filters by resampling frequency. It also defines a query-side HETR.

### Experiments and results

In Table 1, except for the no-expansion row, corrected p > 0.5 for nDCG@10 vs. HyDE, CSQE, MILL; Query2doc p = 0.021. Table 3 macro-average: SAFE-QE unsupported word load 4.79, Query2doc 27.83, HyDE 57.47.

### Limitations and future work

The authors state that τg is fixed and should be calibrated per retriever, component ablations were not done, and limited-sample analysis of breadth and consistency remains open. The experiments use six collections, three retrievers, and four generators; the paper does not report the values of each hyperparameter.
